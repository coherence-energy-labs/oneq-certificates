\title{Certifying quantum error-correction decoders: independently checkable near-optimality receipts at scale}

\OwnerAuthor
\OwnerAffiliation

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
A quantum error-correction decoder returns a correction and nothing else, and syndrome checking cannot detect a valid correction heavier than necessary. For minimum-weight decoding the correction is a minimum-weight $T$-join, whose linear-programming dual is a packing of $T$-odd cuts; any packing, scaled to feasibility, lower-bounds every correction by weak duality, whoever produced it. We build a producer of such packings and a checker that decides in exact rational arithmetic whether a packing proves its correction within $10^{-6}$ of optimal, and keep every receipt. On \EpsSimShots\ simulated shots of a distance-5 surface code over 25 rounds, \EpsSimAcceptedPct\% of corrections were proven. On Google Willow shots decoded by PyMatching on the published error models, \EpsHwGenFourPct\% of \EpsHwGenFourNontrivial\ were proven on ten configurations and \EpsHwGenSixPct\% of \EpsHwGenSixNontrivial\ at distance 7 over 30 rounds, shots the producer was developed on; on held-out 50-round runs fixed in advance, all \HoWDThreeN\ at distance 3, \HoWDFivePct\% of \HoWDFiveN\ at distance 5 and \HoDSevenAbstract. Every receipt replays under two Python verifiers, and a JavaScript checker written by a separate agent from this paper and a logged brief agrees with them on \JsReplayReceipts\ replayed receipts and \FuzzCases\ fuzzed inputs. A sound checker refuses every correction heavier than the optimum by more than $10^{-6}$; what experiment measures is the false-alarm rate, \DetCleanFalseAlarms\ of \DetCleanN\ unmodified corrections. Our producer takes a mean of \CertifyMsMean~ms per shot (median \CertifyMsMedian~ms), about \CostRatioBatch\ times the mean cost of a batched decode; PyMatching's own sparse-blossom dual, exported by a small patch, proves each of its \NdIntShots\ corrections exactly optimal for the integers it decodes with, but only \NdRawProvenTotal\ within $10^{-6}$ on the published weights; decoding, exporting and building that witness takes \NdCallRatioLo--\NdCallRatioHi\ times a plain decode per call, and \NdCompleteRatioLo--\NdCompleteRatioHi\ times with our exact check.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A decoder is software, and software has defects. An assurance argument for a fault-tolerant memory therefore has to answer a concrete question: if the decoder were subtly wrong, what would notice? In current practice the answer is syndrome consistency. A correction is accepted when the set of detectors it makes odd equals the set that fired, a property every functioning decoder satisfies by construction.

Syndrome consistency is blind to the failure that matters most for a minimum-weight decoder: a correction that explains the syndrome but is not the lightest one. A defective priority queue, a stale cache, or a mis-set tie-break can produce exactly such corrections. They pass every syndrome check, and detecting them by benchmarking means resolving a rate change against a noisy baseline, where decoder defects are confounded with device drift.

Minimum-weight decoding admits a better check. The problem is a minimum-weight $T$-join~\cite{edmondsjohnson1973}, its linear-programming dual is a packing of $T$-odd cuts, and weak duality makes any feasible packing a lower bound on every correction, regardless of the algorithm that produced it. A correction whose weight meets such a bound is optimal, and the packing is a witness that a third party can check in time proportional to its size and the edges it touches, without a solver and without trusting the decoder. This is in the spirit of the certifying algorithms of McConnell \textit{et al.}~\cite{mcconnell2011certifying} and the program checkers of Blum and Kannan~\cite{blumkannan1995}, though not complete in their sense: our producer does not find a witness for every correct output.

This paper reports the engineering and measurement needed to make that observation carry weight in practice:
\begin{itemize}
\item a producer of packings whose receipts prove \EpsSimAcceptedPct\% of simulated corrections, and \EpsHwGenFourPct\% (base ladder) and \EpsHwGenSixPct\% (full ladder) of development hardware corrections, within $10^{-6}$ of optimal, with held-out samples fixed in advance (Secs.~\ref{sec:producer} and~\ref{sec:results});
\item an exact $\eps$-checker, a second verifier in scaled-integer arithmetic, a third in JavaScript written by a separate agent from this paper and a logged brief, a differential fuzzer between them, and a forgery battery whose attacks are audited for falsity (Secs.~\ref{sec:checker}, \ref{sec:semantics} and~\ref{sec:results});
\item a precise account of what a receipt proves when weights are IEEE-754 doubles, and a complete rerun of the scale experiments under it with every receipt kept (Secs.~\ref{sec:semantics} and~\ref{sec:results});
\item two detection experiments, on seeded decoder faults and on an upstream calibration fault, where syndrome checking detects nothing: their positive arm follows from weak duality, and what they measure is the false-alarm rate (Sec.~\ref{sec:detection});
\item preregistered per-shot optimality-gap distributions for BP-OSD in two configurations and for belief-matching, with a post hoc $2\times2$ that separates BP-OSD's input model from its min-sum scaling (Sec.~\ref{sec:gaps}); and
\item the cost of certification, including what the decoder's own dual gives (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}).
\end{itemize}
Related work and what this paper does not claim are in Sec.~\ref{sec:related}; the history of how the evidence was produced, including every defect found in it, is in Appendix~\ref{app:log}.

\section{Certificates for minimum-weight decoding}
\label{sec:certificates}

Let $G=(V,E,w)$ be a decoding graph with non-negative edge weights and a virtual boundary vertex $b$, and let $\T\subseteq V$ be the set of fired detectors, with $b$ added when $|\T|$ is odd so that $|\T|$ is even. A correction is an edge set $J\subseteq E$ whose odd-degree vertices are exactly $\T$, a $\T$-join; minimum-weight decoding asks for one of least weight $w(J)=\sum_{e\in J}w_e$~\cite{dennis2002topological,higgott2025sparse}.

A set $S\subseteq V$ is $\T$-odd when $|S\cap\T|$ is odd, and $\delt(S)$ denotes the edges with exactly one endpoint in $S$. Every $\T$-join meets every $\T$-odd cut, which gives the packing problem
\begin{equation}
\max \sum_S z_S \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{S:\,e\in\delt(S)} z_S \le w_e\ \ \forall e\in E,\qquad z\ge 0,
\label{eq:packing}
\end{equation}
over $\T$-odd sets $S$. For any feasible $z$ and any $\T$-join $J$,
\begin{equation}
w(J) \;\ge\; \sum_{e\in J}\sum_{S:\,e\in\delt(S)} z_S \;=\; \sum_S z_S\,|J\cap\delt(S)| \;\ge\; \sum_S z_S ,
\label{eq:weakduality}
\end{equation}
so a packing whose value equals $w(J)$ proves $J$ minimum. Two properties make this a useful certificate. The argument never refers to how $z$ was obtained, so the checker need not trust the producer. And the constraints are edge-local: feasibility is verified by accumulating, per edge, the values of the sets whose boundary it crosses.

Existence is not in question. Edmonds and Johnson showed that the $\T$-cut constraints describe the dominant of the $\T$-join polytope~\cite{edmondsjohnson1973}, so the linear program~\eqref{eq:packing} attains the minimum $\T$-join weight and an attaining packing exists for every syndrome. What the rates below measure is therefore how often our producer finds one, not whether one exists; Sec.~\ref{sec:oracle} checks the decoder's side independently. Node potentials with $y_u+y_v\le w_e$ are the dual of a different primal and are not a $\T$-join certificate; the checker refuses them (Appendix~\ref{app:log}).

\section{The checker}
\label{sec:checker}

Given the graph, the syndrome, a correction and a packing, the checker (i)~verifies that the correction's odd-degree set equals $\T$; (ii)~verifies that every $z_S$ is non-negative and every $S$ is $\T$-odd, since admitting an even set lets the bound exceed the optimum; (iii)~accumulates the load every edge carries; and (iv)~scales the packing by the largest factor, at most one, that makes every edge constraint hold and compares the scaled value with $w(J)$ in exact arithmetic, reporting the difference as the proven slack (Sec.~\ref{sec:semantics}). A value above $w(J)$ is impossible for valid input and is reported as a verifier failure, never accepted as a better bound. Loads are accumulated from each set's boundary, so the work is proportional to the total size of the listed sets and of the edges incident to them, not to the graph size times the number of sets; Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling} shows median check time growing with exponent \CheckExponent\ in the number of edges ($R^2=\CheckRSquared$).

\paragraph*{The input domain.} A checker is part of the trust boundary only if it decides exactly the documents it says it decides. All three implementations below enforce one stated contract: vertex identifiers are integers (the boundary is $-1$), never booleans or floating-point numbers; the graph is simple, so a self-loop or an edge listed twice in either orientation is refused (every decoding graph here is simple, checked on each persisted instance); every weight and packing value is a hexadecimal binary64 literal that names its value exactly, finite and non-negative; every packing set and correction endpoint is a vertex of the graph; and a set or a correction edge listed twice is refused, because a packing is a map from sets to values and a correction is a set of edges. Anything else is refused as invalid input, never coerced. The contract was made one policy on 2026-11-03, when a differential fuzzer showed the Python readers coercing inputs the third implementation refused (below).

\label{fig:scaling}
\end{figure}

\paragraph*{Certificates that travel.} A certificate is useful only if a stranger can re-check it. It is bound to its graph by a fingerprint the verifier recomputes from the graph it is given, so a genuine certificate for an easy instance cannot be replayed against a hard one; every floating-point value travels as an exact hexadecimal literal; and a shot that does not certify still receives a document, labelled not proven, with its slack, so that failure and silence are distinguishable. A signature attests authorship only.

\paragraph*{Second and third implementations.} A checker written by the same author in the same language cannot expose a shared misreading of the problem, and two checkers fed by one parser cannot expose that parser's. The results of Sec.~\ref{sec:results} rest on the $\eps$-checker of Sec.~\ref{sec:semantics}; every receipt is re-decided by a second verifier written separately in scaled-integer arithmetic, in the same language. We therefore also wrote a third, \nolinkurl{verifier-js/verify-epsilon.mjs}. A fresh AI agent wrote it from Secs.~II--IV of this paper and a brief (\nolinkurl{docs/reviews/JS_EPSILON_BRIEF_2026-11-03.md}) that gave the wire format and the kinds of malformed input the fuzzer below would send; the brief's author had read the Python checkers. The writing agent had access to the whole file system: it was told not to open the Python sources and reported that it did not, and the list of the files it opened, released with the brief, is our summary of its report, not a log. It is the same project's work, and what it offers is an independent reading, not independent authorship. A differential fuzzer (\nolinkurl{tools/fuzz_epsilon_checkers.py}) compared the three on random valid receipts over small graphs, decided across $\eps_{\max}$ in both directions, and on one-defect malformed mutants of each. On every valid input the three returned the same status, slack and bound. On malformed input they first split in \FuzzClassesBefore\ classes (\FuzzDisagreementsBefore\ cases), every one rooted in the Python readers and most shared by both Python verifiers: the receipt loader read $0.9$ as vertex $0$ and \texttt{true} as vertex $1$, and read the decimal-looking literal \texttt{0.5} as hexadecimal $0.3125$; the second verifier accepted a self-loop and \texttt{true} as a detector and raised, rather than refused, on a non-finite weight; the first collapsed a parallel edge to the lighter copy. None of these could accept a false claim about a well-formed receipt, but each read a malformed document as some other document, which is the shared-misreading failure the second implementation exists to catch and the same-language pair could not. After the fix all three agree on \FuzzCases\ fuzzed inputs (\FuzzValidCases\ valid), every class is a standing conformance vector (\EpsVectors\ vectors, \EpsVectorClasses\ classes) run against all three, and the JavaScript checker returns the recorded verdict, slack and bound on \JsReplayReceipts\ replayed receipts from \JsReplayConfigs\ released configurations and Sec.~\ref{sec:measured-model}.

The earlier JavaScript checker, \nolinkurl{verifier-js/verify-certificate.mjs}, implements the exact checker only, and it is less independent than first described. Its parser, graph binding, $\T$-join test and $\T$-oddness test (with $b$ counted in $\T$, Sec.~\ref{sec:certificates}) were written from the specification; its exact-arithmetic acceptance step was ported from the Python checker and kept identical to it, and the two shared at least one misreading of boundary edges for a month (Appendix~\ref{app:log}). It and the exact checker agree on all \ConfVectors\ conformance vectors, \ConfRefusals\ of which are refusals; agreement on the ported step is not independent evidence.

\section{What a receipt proves under floating-point weights}
\label{sec:semantics}

Decoding weights are log-likelihood ratios stored as IEEE-754 doubles, and packings are computed in floating point. Exact equality between a packing's value and $w(J)$ is therefore rarely observable, and a checker must decide what to accept. The choice changes the claim, so we separate two claims:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{exact}: $w(J)=\min_{J'} w(J')$ over the double-valued weights, exactly;
\item \emph{$\eps$-optimal}: $w(J)-\min_{J'} w(J')\le\eps$, with $\eps$ proven.
\end{itemize}
Every double is a rational number, so either claim can be decided in exact arithmetic. The following lemma turns any non-negative packing over $\T$-odd sets, feasible or slightly overloaded, into a proven slack.

\begin{lemma}
Let $z\ge0$ be supported on $\T$-odd sets, let $\ell_e=\sum_{S:\,e\in\delt(S)}z_S$, and let $\theta=\min\bigl(1,\min_{e:\,\ell_e>0} w_e/\ell_e\bigr)$. Then $\theta z$ is feasible for~\eqref{eq:packing}, and for every $\T$-join $J$ with minimum $J^\star$,
\[
w(J)-w(J^\star)\;\le\;\eps(J,z)\;:=\;w(J)-\theta\sum_S z_S .
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Each constraint of $\theta z$ reads $\theta\ell_e\le w_e$, which holds by the choice of $\theta$. Applying~\eqref{eq:weakduality} to $\theta z$ and $J^\star$ gives $w(J^\star)\ge\theta\sum_S z_S$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{The checker the results use.} The $\eps$-checker (\texttt{check\_epsilon}) decides the $\eps$-claim itself. It converts every weight and dual value to an exact rational; refuses input outside the domain of Sec.~\ref{sec:checker}; computes $\theta$ and $\eps(J,z)$ exactly; and returns one of five outcomes: \emph{exactly optimal} ($\eps=0$), \emph{$\eps$-optimal} ($0<\eps\le\eps_{\max}$, with $\eps$ reported), \emph{not proven} ($\eps>\eps_{\max}$), \emph{invalid input}, or \emph{verifier failure}, a bound above the correction's weight, impossible for valid input and never an acceptance. A refusal is not evidence that the decoder erred; only a lighter valid correction is. The headline rates of Sec.~\ref{sec:results} are acceptances of this checker with $\eps_{\max}=10^{-6}$, from complete reruns that kept every receipt, and every receipt is re-decided by the second verifier, which reads the receipt's lists itself, and the replay stops on the first receipt where the two, or either and the recorded verdict, disagree. The checker's tests kill \MutEpsKilled\ of \MutEpsRun\ mutants generated mechanically by standard mutation operators; each of the remaining \MutEpsEquivalent\ computes the same function as the original, for a reason recorded with the score.

\paragraph*{The rule the original runs used.} The runs first made for this work accepted a certificate when its floating-point gap was at most $10^{-6}$ and no edge was overloaded by more than $10^{-9}$. An external audit showed that such a rule is not a proof: it accepts a correspondingly small suboptimality (a correction of weight $1.0000005$ was accepted against a true optimum of $1$), it had no guard against non-finite values, and its checker never tested that a packing's sets are $\T$-odd (Appendix~\ref{app:log}). Those runs did not keep their certificates, and their rates are kept in Sec.~\ref{sec:results} only as a development record, labelled as tolerance-rule acceptances wherever they appear. To measure what the rule actually certified, we re-ran the exact code that produced each headline artifact, extracted from its commit, on seeded samples of six configurations, kept every certificate it accepted, and analysed each in exact rational arithmetic (Table~\ref{tab:semantics}). None of the \AuditAccepted\ accepted certificates was inadmissible, and the largest proven slack was \AuditEpsMax. For the runs it sampled, that bounds the inadmissible share of their acceptances below \AuditBoundSimPct\% for the simulation, \AuditBoundWFiveXPct\% for Willow at distance 5 over 13 rounds (X) and \AuditBoundWSevenXPct\% at distance 7 over 30 rounds (X), one-sided 95\% each; no other configuration or generation was sampled.

\paragraph*{Exact certification is a different and harder claim.} The exact checker, \texttt{matching\_cert.check}, accepts only a proof of exact optimality: it repairs the floating-point rounding of a packing in exact arithmetic and then verifies feasibility and attainment from scratch. It accepts \AuditExactSame\ of the \AuditAccepted\ certificates above (\AuditExactSamePct\%), and the fraction falls steeply with graph size (Table~\ref{tab:semantics}). Part of the refusals are the decoder's own: PyMatching matches on internally discretised weights, and among the \DetCleanN\ unmodified corrections of Sec.~\ref{sec:detection}, \DetCleanPymExcessPositive\ exceeded the exact optimum over the double-valued weights by a positive amount of at most $10^{-6}$. Because a decoder that computes in floating point is not attempting exact optimality over double-valued weights, and because those weights are estimates far coarser than $10^{-13}$, we regard $\eps$-optimality with a small, proven $\eps$ as the operationally meaningful claim, and report exact certification separately.

\section{Producing the witness}
\label{sec:producer}

Producing a packing is the expensive half. \emph{Moat growth} grows every $\T$-odd component at equal rate and merges components when an edge becomes tight, as in the primal--dual approximation of Goemans and Williamson~\cite{goemans1995general}; unlike Edmonds' exact blossom algorithm~\cite{edmonds1965paths}, it does not revise its choices. It is feasible by construction and \GrowthSpeedupLo--\GrowthSpeedupHi\ times faster than solving a linear program, but at distance~7 it certifies \GrowthDSevenCertified\ shots against \LpDSevenCertified\ for the linear program, on the same shots (tolerance rule, development), because greedy merging picks the wrong pairs.

The main producer solves~\eqref{eq:packing} restricted to a candidate family of sets. Restricting the family cannot cost soundness, since a feasible point of the restricted problem is feasible for the full one; it costs only tightness, which appears as a reported gap. The groups worth adding are read from the packing's own slack: a set can carry more value only if every edge on its boundary has slack, so the connected components of the subgraph of tight edges are added and the problem solved again. The producer is a ladder whose rungs are tried cheapest first, escalating only on shots the previous rung left open: tight-component refinement; sets that cut the correction exactly once, as complementary slackness requires of an optimal dual; candidates translated from the duals of a cutting-plane linear program for perfect matching on the metric closure over $\T$; an escalation tier that raises every binding budget on refused shots; and a final exact tier that generates $\T$-odd cuts by Padberg--Rao separation~\cite{padbergrao1982}, capped at a round budget. Why the translated candidates leave some shots open for the later tiers is not determined: we have not measured which of the cutting-plane program's non-convergence, the quantisation of its values, the per-detector boundary copies of the metric closure, or the candidate budget dominates on the shots they leave open. The correction never changes between rungs, and every rung is judged by the same checker. We call the rungs up to the closure candidates the \emph{base ladder}, and the base ladder followed by the escalation and exact tiers the \emph{full ladder}. None of these rungs reads the dual the decoder itself computed; Sec.~\ref{sec:cost} measures what that dual gives.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All rates are rounded down. An interval is the exact two-sided 95\% Clopper--Pearson interval; a bound stated alone is the exact one-sided 95\% Clopper--Pearson upper bound.

\subsection{Simulation at scale}

We sampled \EpsSimShots\ shots of a distance-5 rotated surface-code memory over 25 rounds at circuit-level depolarising noise $p=0.005$, generated with Stim~\cite{gidney2021stim} and decoded with PyMatching~\cite{higgott2025sparse} on a graph of \SimDetectors\ detectors plus the boundary and \SimEdges\ edges, following the seed plan of the original run chunk for chunk. Each correction went through the base ladder and was judged by the $\eps$-checker, every chunk completed (\EpsSimChunkRetries\ retries), and every receipt was kept. Of \EpsSimNontrivial\ nontrivial corrections, \EpsSimAccepted\ (\EpsSimAcceptedPct\%) were proven within $\eps_{\max}=10^{-6}$ of optimal, \EpsSimExact\ of them exactly, with a largest proven slack of \EpsSimLargestEps; the remaining \EpsSimNotProven\ (\EpsSimNotProvenPct\%) were not proven. On \FreshSimN\ further shots of the same configuration from seeds far from every recorded seed base, drawn after the first review round of this manuscript, \FreshSimAccepted\ (\FreshSimPct\%; \FreshSimCILo--\FreshSimCIHi\%) were proven and \FreshSimNotProven\ were not (post hoc). The same experiment under the tolerance rule accepted \SimCertified\ of \SimShots\ corrections (\SimCertifiedPct\%), from chunks with memory failures replaced by supplements (Appendix~\ref{app:log}); the rerun has no such gap.

\paragraph*{The forgery battery, and what its zero meant.} For every shot with a packing we built forged certificates from the strongest packing found: corrections with a dropped, duplicated or mismatched edge; a strictly heavier cheapest-boundary correction; three ``strictly heavier'' corrections meant to reach the bound (a spurious cycle, the packing inflated to meet the heavier weight, and the same with a negative value hiding the inflation); and the genuine packing with one value replaced by NaN or by $+\infty$. In the scale reruns the three stealth classes did not do what they were for: each listed one edge twice, which as an edge set is the genuine correction, and the $\eps$-checker refuses a repeated edge as malformed input before computing any bound. Of the \EpsSimForgeries\ forgeries on the simulation, \EpsSimForgeriesSyndrome\ failed the syndrome or repeated-edge test, \EpsSimForgeriesDoubled\ were these doubled-edge documents, \EpsSimForgeriesNonFinite\ carried a non-finite value, and \EpsSimForgeriesBound, the one cheapest-boundary correction, reached weak duality. The battery now adds an even cycle disjoint from the correction, a valid and strictly heavier edge set; keeps the doubled-edge documents under their true name; and its audit refuses an attack whose correction lists an edge twice. Run over \ReplayStrict\ released receipt chunk in the strict replay (\ReplayForgeries\ forgeries, \ReplayForgeriesBound\ of them refused by the bound), over Sec.~\ref{sec:measured-model} (\CertMeasForgeries) and over the IBM run (\IbmEpsForgeries), no forgery was accepted. The linear-programming adversary that found the one real checker defect (packings over sets that are not $\T$-odd once $b$ is counted in $\T$; Appendix~\ref{app:log}) is part of that battery: on each attacked receipt it builds a strictly heavier correction ``proven'' by such sets, its packing scaled so that the bound lands within $\eps_{\max}$ below the heavier weight. Whether such a forgery is real is decided by running it through copies of both verifiers with only their parity test removed: of its \ReplayParity\ forgeries in the strict replay, both parity-blind copies accepted \ReplayParityFool, and both real verifiers refused every one as invalid input. Unscaled, as the search first returns it, the packing's bound exceeds the heavier weight, and a parity-blind checker reports that as impossible rather than accepting it. On a sample of replayed receipts in the test suite a parity-blind copy of the JavaScript checker accepts the scaled forgeries too and the real one refuses them. The replay also attacks the acceptance boundary: one correction edge's weight is raised to the smallest double that puts the proven slack past $\eps_{\max}$, which both verifiers must refuse, or by half the remaining room, which both must accept; and receipts are replayed against another configuration's graph, where none may be accepted. These counts show that the attacks tried were refused; that no forgery can succeed is what the lemma says.

\subsection{Is the decoder, or the producer, responsible for refusals?}
\label{sec:oracle}

A refusal can mean the decoder's correction was not optimal or that the producer failed to find a tight packing. To separate the two we computed, for every nontrivial shot of three simulated configurations, a minimum-weight perfect matching on the metric closure over $\T$ with per-detector boundary copies, using networkx~\cite{hagberg2008networkx}, a different formulation and algorithm sharing no code with the producer. On all \OracleShots\ shots (\OracleShotsThree\ at distance 3 over \OracleRoundsThree\ rounds, \OracleShotsFive\ at distance 5 and \OracleShotsSeven\ at distance 7, both over 25 rounds) the oracle's weight equalled the decoder's to floating-point resolution, far inside $\eps_{\max}$, so every refusal on those shots was producer slack; Sec.~\ref{sec:detection} repeats the separation with an exact-arithmetic oracle.

\label{sec:other-hw}

\subsection{Certifying against a model measured from the run itself}
\label{sec:measured-model}

Every run above certifies against weights supplied from outside the shots being decoded. A certificate proves minimum weight with respect to the weights it is given and is indifferent to where they came from, so the question here is narrower than it may look: whether the accept path runs unchanged on a model estimated from the device's own syndromes, the form a deployed system would want. The estimator is not ours: the two-point estimator that recovers pairwise error probabilities from detector correlations alone is due to Spitz \textit{et al.}~\cite{spitz2018adaptive}, the analysis Google applied to repetition codes~\cite{chen2021exponential}, and Krishnamoorthy \textit{et al.} have decoded IBM hardware shots under ``a \emph{learned} model on the same graph whose priors are re-fitted from the recorded detector statistics by the standard two-point correlation estimator'' (\cite{krishnamoorthy2026certified}, Sec.~8.6: a distance-3 surface code over three rounds on \texttt{ibm\_kingston}, where the re-fitted model made the logical flip rate slightly worse, and where ``the AIS decoder certifies $96\%$ of its decisions'', certificates of maximum-likelihood optimality under the assumed model that are statistical, a paired bootstrap on annealed-importance-sampling weights (their Sec.~7.4)).

We estimated a decoding graph from the first half of each run's detectors and certified the second half against it, on \CertMeasRecords\ preregistered hardware records across \CertMeasDevices\ IBM processors, at distances 3 and 5 and at 13 and 40 rounds, \CertMeasHeldOut\ held-out shots in total, on graphs of \CertMeasEdgesLo\ to \CertMeasEdgesHi\ edges. Of \CertMeasNontrivial\ nontrivial held-out shots the exact checker proved \CertMeasCertified\ exactly optimal (the \CertMeasRefused\ refusals all on the two 40-round records) and the $\eps$-checker proved \CertMeasEpsAccepted\ within $10^{-6}$. Every receipt is kept with its estimated graph (\nolinkurl{evidence/certified_measured_receipts/}), both verifiers and the JavaScript checker return the same verdict on every one, and none of \CertMeasForgeries\ forgeries was accepted, \CertMeasBoundForgeries\ of them refused by the bound. The accept path and the campaign's own scorer return identical logical-error counts on every record.

We do not report how much better the estimated graph decodes than the models it replaces: on these short repetition-code records the baseline models' logical error rates are poor enough that the comparison would say more about the baselines than about the estimator, and estimated models outperforming device-informed ones is documented elsewhere, including on IBM hardware~\cite{takou2026logical}, where a calibration-conditioned neural decoder beats calibration-weighted matching by up to $11.1\times$ at distance 11 on these same processors~\cite{stein2026calibration}; no priority is claimed for the estimator or for any decoding margin it brings. Certifying against a measured model does not make the measured model true; it makes the decoder's answer checkable under that model, and nothing more.

\section{What the certificate detects}
\label{sec:detection}

\subsection{Seeded decoder faults}

What a certificate can detect is fixed in advance by the lemma: a packing proves at most the optimum, so no packing certifies a correction heavier than the optimum by more than $\eps_{\max}$, and a sound checker refuses every such correction. The experiments below therefore do not measure detection power in the usual sense. They test that the implemented checker behaves as the lemma says on realistic faults, and they measure what the lemma cannot fix: the false-alarm rate, the refusals of corrections that are in fact within $\eps_{\max}$ of optimal. The lighter correction that proves a refused one suboptimal comes from an exact oracle, not from the certificate; a certificate alone says ``not proven'', and only a lighter valid correction says ``suboptimal''.

We mutated the decoder's output at distances 3, 5 and 7 with defects of several kinds, which models wrong answers but not crashes, hangs, or memory errors, and judged every correction against the exact minimum-weight correction, computed by an exact-arithmetic oracle whose answer is re-verified before use. Each correction receives one outcome under the $\eps$-checker: \emph{invalid}, when it does not explain the syndrome; \emph{certified}; \emph{proven suboptimal}, when it is refused and a lighter valid correction is exhibited; or \emph{not proven}, when it is refused without such a witness. Of \DetDefectN\ defective corrections, \DetDefectInvalid\ were invalid, which syndrome checking also detects, and the other \DetDefectProven\ were proven suboptimal; none was certified and none was left not proven. Those \DetStealthN\ are the informative subset: faults constructed to leave the syndrome exactly satisfied while making the correction strictly heavier, invisible to syndrome checking by construction. On the \DetCleanN\ unmodified corrections of the same shots, the base ladder refused \DetCleanFalseAlarms\ (\CIFaBaseLo--\CIFaBaseHi\%) and the full ladder \DetCleanFalseAlarmsFull\ (at most \CIFaFullHi\%), and every such refusal was producer slack on a correction within $\eps_{\max}$ of optimal. As a negative control, all \DetControlN\ equal-weight alternative optima, generated at distances 3 and 5 (none arose at distance 7), were certified (a false-alarm rate of at most \CICtrlHi\% on them). The released corpus of \FaultCorpus\ seeded cases holds these \DetDefectN\ defective corrections, the \DetControlN\ controls, and \DetDefectNonDefective\ mutation whose output turned out not to be defective, so other detectors can be scored on the same faults.

This experiment is open to the objection that the mutations were chosen knowing what the certificate checks. The next one is not, although it needs a reference of its own.

\subsection{A stale calibration}

Here the decoder runs unmodified and its output is never touched. The fault is upstream: the decoder decodes on a graph built from a drifted error model, as it would if its calibration snapshot were stale, and its corrections are checked against a reference model supplied independently, here the model that generated the data. A drift factor $f$ multiplies each error mechanism's probability by an independent factor $e^{U}$, with $U$ uniform on $[-\ln f,\ln f]$, clipped to $[10^{-12},0.49]$, and the decoder's matching graph is rebuilt from the drifted model. Every correction is syndrome-consistent by construction, because the decoder solves a correct problem with wrong weights.

What the certificate adds deserves a precise statement. With the reference model in hand, comparing the two models detects that they differ, without any certificate. What it does not say is which decisions the difference changed, or by how much. The certificate answers that per shot, without running a second decoder: it flags exactly the corrections the reference does not prove near-optimal. Turning a flag into ``proven suboptimal'' still needs a lighter correction, which here comes from the exact oracle under the reference, and the certificate cannot say which of the two models describes the device.

\section{Per-shot optimality gaps}
\label{sec:gaps}

Decoders are usually compared by logical error rate, an average that cannot distinguish a decoder that is consistently slightly suboptimal from one that is usually exact with a heavy tail. A certified correction on the same shot makes each decoder's excess over the minimum measurable, and we measured it in an experiment fixed before it ran (\nolinkurl{docs/PREREG_gap_distribution_2026-11-03.md}): \GapShotsPerDistance\ shots per distance $d\in\{3,5,7\}$, rounds equal to distance, $p=0.005$, from seeds \GapSeeds\ that no earlier run used.

The reference is PyMatching's correction $J$, proven by the base ladder and the $\eps$-checker within $\eps(J)\le10^{-6}$ of optimal on \GapEpsRef\ of \GapNontrivial\ nontrivial shots. The optimum then lies in $[w(J)-\eps(J),w(J)]$, so for another decoder's correction $K$ the excess $w(K)-w(J^\star)$ lies in $[\max(0,g),\,g+\eps(J)]$ with $g=w(K)-w(J)$, computed in exact rationals of the binary64 weights the checker reads. $K$ is \emph{proven within $\eps_{\max}$ of optimal} when the upper end is at most $10^{-6}$ and a \emph{proven miss} when the lower end exceeds it; no shot fell between. A decoder's selection is projected onto graph edges modulo 2 before it is weighed, since a correction is an edge set. Three decoders were run. BP-OSD~\cite{panteleev2021degenerate,roffe2020decoding} (ldpc 2.4.1~\cite{roffe2022ldpc} \texttt{BpOsdDecoder}: min-sum, 30 iterations, parallel schedule, OSD-CS of order 5) in two configurations: one column per matching-graph edge, with the XOR-combined probability of the graphlike components on it, which reproduces PyMatching's merged weights to \GapMergeRelDiff\ relative and so gives BP-OSD the matching graph's objective, with min-sum scaling $0.625$ (the preregistered primary arm); and one column per graphlike component of each decomposed error, components treated as independent, with the library's default unscaled min-sum (the configuration of this section's earlier version, now a sensitivity arm, which the preregistration calls an input-model one). The two BP-OSD arms therefore differ in two factors at once, the input model and the min-sum scaling, and comparing them compares two configurations; a post hoc $2\times2$ below separates the factors. And belief-matching~\cite{higgott2023improved}, which matches under weights informed by belief propagation. Neither BP-OSD configuration is a maximum-likelihood decoder, and a gap is weight, not homology class.

\section{Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

We timed the base ladder on the configuration of the simulation at scale, distance 5 over 25 rounds at $p=\CostP$, on \CostShots\ nontrivial shots from that run's own seed plan; every verdict matched the stored receipt for the same shot. Certification took a median of \CertifyMsMedian~ms per shot, a mean of \CertifyMsMean~ms and a 99th percentile of \CertifyMsPNinetyNine~ms; the \CertifyRefusedN\ refused shots took a median of about \CertifyRefusedMsMedian~ms each. Against PyMatching on the same graph, a mean of \DecodeUsBatch~$\mu$s per shot batched and \DecodeUsPerShot~$\mu$s per call, the mean cost of certification is about \CostRatioBatch\ and \CostRatioPerShot\ times the mean cost of decoding. Checking a certificate with the $\eps$-checker took a mean of \CheckMsMean~ms (median \CheckMsMedian~ms, 99th percentile \CheckMsPNinetyNine~ms), about \CostRatioCheckBatch\ times the mean batched decode. These are single-thread Python times on a shared workstation whose processors were \CostCpuLoadPct--\CostCpuLoadAfterPct\% busy when sampled; the ratios were measured in the same run.

\paragraph*{What the decoder's own dual gives.} Most of that cost is the producer's, not the problem's. Sparse blossom maintains, and then discards, a dual of exactly the form the checker reads: its frozen region radii are nested $\T$-odd sets whose values sum to the matching weight. A patch of about 150 lines (\nolinkurl{third_party/pymatching_return_duals.patch}) exports it. We rebuilt it on the machine of this paper and ran it on \NdSimN\ shots of the simulation's seed plan and the first \NdWFiveN\ and \NdWSevenN\ shots of two Willow development configurations (distance 5 and 7 over 30 rounds, X), in two arms (\nolinkurl{experiments/m0_prior_art/pymatching_native_dual.py} and \nolinkurl{pymatching_native_dual_arms.py}). In the first, PyMatching decodes the published weights, which it discretises internally to the integers $2\,\mathrm{round}(w c)$, $c=(2^{24}-1)/\max_e w_e$; checked exactly against those integers, its exported dual proves every one of the \NdIntShots\ corrections exactly optimal, and the scale the decoder reports equals $2c$ on every shot. In the second, we rounded the weights ourselves onto a dyadic grid ($2^{-\NdSimGridBits}$, $2^{-\NdWFiveGridBits}$, $2^{-\NdWSevenGridBits}$) and scaled them to integers, so that PyMatching's integer problem is the grid problem; there too its dual proves every correction exactly optimal. On every shot the two arms return the same correction, and it is the correction this paper certifies for that shot; as a control, the certified correction with one edge dropped is told apart from both on every shot. Neither dual is a proof on the published doubles. Divided by its scale and judged there, the first arm's dual has proven slack with medians \NdSimRawEpsMedian, \NdWFiveRawEpsMedian\ and \NdWSevenRawEpsMedian\ and a maximum of \NdRawEpsMaxAll, so under $\eps_{\max}=10^{-6}$ it proves \NdSimRawProven, \NdWFiveRawProven\ and \NdWSevenRawProven\ of the shots; the second arm's dual, judged there, has medians \NdSimGridOnRawEpsMedian, \NdWFiveGridOnRawEpsMedian\ and \NdWSevenGridOnRawEpsMedian, a maximum of \NdGridOnRawEpsMaxAll, and proves \NdGridOnRawProvenTotal. Per call, on the same calls, we timed the witness's production, its check and the two together. A decoder-native dual therefore yields a witness of exactness for the integers the decoder actually decodes with at \NdCallRatioLo--\NdCallRatioHi\ times a plain decode per call, and on this route the exact check, as our Python checker does it, is most of the cost of the certified call (on the decoder's integers the same check needs no rationals; no faster checker was measured); the linear-programming ladder is what buys $10^{-6}$ on the published doubles, and its cost ratios above are a property of that producer, not of certification. With current producers at $\eps_{\max}=10^{-6}$, certification suits offline audit, acceptance testing of decoder releases, and spot-checking a sampled fraction of a live stream.

\section{Related work and scope}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph*{Certifying and checking.} Certifying algorithms~\cite{mcconnell2011certifying} and program checkers~\cite{blumkannan1995} are classical, as is the linear-programming structure of matching and $\T$-joins~\cite{edmonds1965polyhedron,edmondsjohnson1973}. In exact integer programming, the gap between a floating-point solver's claim and a proof is well studied, with safe dual bounds and verifiable certificates~\cite{steffy2013valid,cheung2017verifying,eifler2023computational}; Sec.~\ref{sec:semantics} addresses the same issue for decoding receipts.

\paragraph*{Decoding.} Wu \textit{et al.}~\cite{wu2025minimum} formulate most-likely-error decoding as a minimum-weight parity factor problem and decode with a primal--dual algorithm that emits a proximity bound with every solution, so a decoder that produces its own optimality certificate per shot already exists, and Sec.~\ref{sec:cost} shows PyMatching's sparse blossom holds one too. What differs here is the setting: a certificate for a third-party decoder's correction, decided by separately written checkers in exact arithmetic over the published weights, with the cost of producing it measured. Linear-programming decoding of classical and quantum codes has a long history~\cite{feldman2005lp,li2018lp}.

\paragraph*{Concurrent work.} Krishnamoorthy \textit{et al.}~\cite{krishnamoorthy2026certified} decide degenerate-coset maximum likelihood on surface and bivariate-bicycle codes. Their certificates are of two kinds: a probabilistic one, a paired bootstrap, and a deterministic one by mini-bucket elimination. On the $[[72,12,6]]$ code their exact decoding costs 5 to 26~s per syndrome, and mini-bucket elimination at $i$-bounds 16 and 20 reproduces the exact decision on 40 of 40 syndromes and deterministically certifies 37 and 38 of them at a fraction of that cost; on the $[[144,12,12]]$ code their mini-bucket run produced no deterministic certificates, at about 272~s per decode. Their objective is coset maximum likelihood, not the minimum-weight correction certified here, so the two kinds of receipt answer different questions. Shen and Zhong~\cite{shen2026capability} test decoder libraries for conformance and release an oracle-free suboptimality certificate, two feasible corrections of different weight, re-derived by a standalone verifier; that is the witness this paper calls ``proven suboptimal'' (Sec.~\ref{sec:detection}), and the lower bound that certifies optimality is what the receipts here add to it. Ehatamm \textit{et al.}~\cite{ehatamm2026end} formalise quantum error correction end to end in a proof assistant, verifying the framework rather than individual instances. Our literature searches, whose queries are logged with the released artifacts, did not find per-shot $\eps$-optimality receipts for a matching decoder on hardware data at this scale, kept and replayed; not finding them is not proof of absence.

\paragraph*{What is not claimed.} The rates are properties of our producer, a decoder and an error model: they measure how often the producer proves the decoder's answer near-optimal for the given weights, not logical accuracy, hardware fidelity, or real-time readiness; soundness is a property of the checker alone. Certification with our producer is not a real-time decoder (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}). It is not complete: \EpsSimNotProvenPct\% of simulated corrections and \EpsHwNotProven\ hardware corrections were not proven, and those receive receipts stating their proven slack. Exact optimality is counted separately and not claimed at scale. A certificate proves optimality for the weights it was given and says nothing about whether those weights describe the device. Nor can it separate tied optima: a decoder change that selects a different equal-weight correction, possibly in a different logical class, passes unchanged, as the negative control of Sec.~\ref{sec:detection} shows by design.

\section{Reproducibility and data}
\label{sec:repro}

Code, artifacts, conformance vectors, the seeded-fault corpus and the certificate schema are released under the Apache License 2.0 at \url{https://github.com/coherence-energy-labs/oneq-certificates} (release \ReleaseTag). Every receipt of Sec.~\ref{sec:results}, simulated and hardware, the held-out samples and the receipts of Sec.~\ref{sec:measured-model} included, is attached to that release as compressed per-chunk files, each chunk's SHA-256 pinned beside its summary, and one command replays them all with both Python verifiers; the JavaScript checker replays the same files. Every measured number in this paper is typeset from a macro generated from a JSON artifact, a gate in the repository traces every number in the source to the evidence tree, and each artifact records the command and the bytes of the code that produced it; a number is typeset only from bytes that a commit holds. The Willow data are read in place from the published archive~\cite{google2024data}.

Inputs are platform-dependent at the last bit. With identical package versions, PyMatching's merged weights differed in the last place on several edges of the distance-3 graph between a Linux machine and the Windows machine of this paper, and Stim's seeded sampler does not reproduce samples across platforms. Every receipt therefore ships with its graph, an instance file whose hash the receipt's summary carries, and every per-shot record of Sec.~\ref{sec:gaps} with its syndrome; a replay never rebuilds either.

\paragraph*{Use of AI tools.} AI tools assisted with software development and manuscript preparation, and wrote the third checker of Sec.~\ref{sec:checker} from a logged brief. Reviews of this manuscript by AI agents that had not written it, and two external audits of the release, are archived with it; they are not third-party peer review. \section{Verification log}
\label{app:log}

This appendix records how the evidence above was produced and every defect found in it, so that the body can state each claim once.

\paragraph*{A dual that looks right and is not.} Node potentials with $y_u+y_v\le w_e$ constrain adjacent detectors only, so two detectors joined by a path rather than an edge are unconstrained. In development, run against PyMatching at distance 7, such a ``bound'' exceeded the weight of the correction it was shown; the checker refuses any bound above the primal as impossible, and refuses a certificate built from node potentials outright.

\paragraph*{A repeated set.} Writing the first JavaScript checker exposed an ambiguity: given a packing that listed the same vertex set twice with different values, the Python checker deserialised it into a dictionary, kept the last value and accepted, while the JavaScript checker summed the list, found an edge constraint violated, and refused. Both now refuse a repeated set, and the case is a permanent conformance vector.

\paragraph*{What of the first JavaScript checker is independent.} From the repository's history: it was written from the specification on 2026-07-31 (commit \texttt{7a5a3cff}); the exact lattice rule was ported from the Python checker on 2026-08-04 (\texttt{888b7454}); the dual repair that replaced it, and its later spread-absorb and four-order variants, were written into both languages in the same commits on 2026-08-05 (\texttt{6b3a146d}, \texttt{e0767dc7}, \texttt{900d6471}), deliberately identical; and boundary-first edge keys failed to canonicalise in both until \texttt{56dce450} (2026-08-18). Its header, which said nothing in it was ported, now says this.

\paragraph*{A defect in the exact checkers, found and fixed.} Until 2026-10-18 the exact checker (\texttt{matching\_cert.check}), in every version including the one that applied the tolerance rule in the original runs, never tested that every set of a packing is $\T$-odd; a stricter variant tested oddness against the fired detectors alone, without the boundary that $\T$ includes when their number is odd. Forged certificates that a linear-programming search over small graphs constructed exploit both: packings over sets that are not $\T$-join cut constraints, certifying corrections of weight 6, 7 and 5 against optima of 4, 6 and 4. The exact checker accepted all three and the stricter variant the first two; both were fixed in commit \texttt{617da3af}, and the three are conformance vectors. The JavaScript checker always refused them, so the two implementations had disagreed on these inputs, and no vector contained one. The $\eps$-checker has tested $\T$-oddness, with $b$ counted, since its first version, so no headline rate depends on the defect. Every count an exact checker decided was recomputed with the fixed checkers and is unchanged: the exact-optimality columns of Table~\ref{tab:semantics} and the certified counts of Sec.~\ref{sec:measured-model}.

\paragraph*{The input domain, made one policy.} The differential fuzzer of Sec.~\ref{sec:checker} found the Python readers coercing malformed input on 2026-11-03; the receipt loader, the $\eps$-checker's correction reader and the second verifier were fixed that day, every class became a standing vector, and every artifact the fix touched was re-run: the strict replay of every released receipt, the IBM re-certification, the semantics audit of Table~\ref{tab:semantics}, the mutation score, and the producer-identity comparison. The preregistered gap run had been judged by the checker as committed before the fix; re-judged by the fixed checker it gives the same verdict, slack and bound on every shot.

\paragraph*{A substituted graph that was the same graph.} The replay's substitution attack re-decides a receipt against another configuration's graph, where nothing may be accepted, and took the first other configuration by name. Once the fresh-seed rerun of the simulation was added, that was the simulation's own circuit, and the strict replay of the simulation reported every substituted receipt accepted. The attack now takes the first configuration whose graph differs, and records which; every other configuration's choice was already a different graph and is unchanged.

\paragraph*{The forgery battery's heavier classes.} Before 2026-11-03 the three classes meant to reach the bound appended one edge twice to the genuine correction; the battery's own falsity audit summed the doubled weight and called the claim false. Read as an edge set the claim was true, and the $\eps$-checker refused the documents as malformed input (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). The audit now refuses such an attack, and a mutant that removes that refusal is killed by the test suite.

\paragraph*{The original simulation run.} The tolerance-rule simulation lost \SimFailedChunks\ worker chunks (\SimShotsLostToChunks\ shots) to memory exhaustion on a shared workstation and was completed from supplements with disjoint seeds. Some of those deaths were allocation failures during certification, whose memory use depends on the syndrome, so its rate cannot exclude selection by difficulty; the rerun has no such gap.

\paragraph*{Development attacks that were not attacks.} Twice during development an ``attack'' was accepted and the checker was right: once the forged correction was an equal-weight alternative optimum, and once a construction meant to hide a negative value degenerated into a valid certificate for an optimal correction. Every forgery other than the non-finite ones is therefore re-verified to be false, independently of the checker, before its verdict is counted, and a run in which any forgery fails that test is void.

\paragraph*{The two open distance-7 shots.} Generation 6 left one shot per basis open. We located both in the chunk records; an exact-arithmetic oracle, whose answer is re-verified, finds PyMatching's correction exactly optimal on each (\HwResidualDetectorsX\ and \HwResidualDetectorsZ\ detectors fired), and the current exact tier stops on both at its cap of \HwResidualRounds\ separation rounds without converging. Generation 6 cost \HwCoreSecX\ s (X) and \HwCoreSecZ\ s (Z) of core time per shot.

\paragraph*{The held-out distance-7 stop.} The run started on 2026-11-03 at 14:35:29 from a snapshot of commit \texttt{2008c81f}, arm after arm as committed. At 16:18:10, 5.13 core-hours in, it was stopped for the blind order amendment (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}), losing the three chunks then in flight, and restarted at 16:29 under the deviation's driver (commit \texttt{4030f88d}) with the deadline unchanged. The watchdog stopped it at 03:55:29 on 2026-11-04, the 40 core-hours of the original start, with \HoDSevenUnfinishedChunks\ chunks being computed; nobody read a not-proven count before the stop record was written. Its \HoDSevenMarkers\ dispatch markers are the \HoDSevenDispatched\ chunks dispatched over the whole run less the \HoDSevenPreDeviationChunks\ completed before the deviation, which the committed runner dispatched without markers; a supervisor that would have restarted the driver had it died (\nolinkurl{heldout_d7_babysitter.py}) never had to act.
